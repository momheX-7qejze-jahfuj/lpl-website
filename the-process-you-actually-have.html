The Process You Actually Have

Pressure does not break your process. It shows you which one was real.

By Coach Joe (Joseph Clementi) · The Monday Brief · 5 min read

The binder is beautiful.

Every car gets a multipoint inspection. Every inspection gets photos. Every estimate goes out inside twenty minutes. Every customer hears from someone before noon.

You wrote it. You trained it. You posted it on the wall.

On a Thursday at two o'clock, the store runs it exactly that way.

Then Monday arrives.

By 8:15 there are eighteen cars on the lot and two advisors on the drive. A technician called out. The loaner board is empty. A customer from Friday stands at the counter holding a question nobody wants.

The inspections still happen. They just happen faster. A tech glances, clicks, and moves on. The photos thin out. The estimate goes at 11:40 instead of 8:40. The call before noon becomes a text at four.

Nobody broke a rule. Nobody stopped caring.

The process did exactly what it was built to do. It was never built for Monday.

PRESSURE DOES NOT CREATE THE GAP. IT REVEALS IT.

Here is what most leaders miss. Monday did not damage your process. Monday showed you your process.

The version that runs on a slow Thursday is the one you wrote. The version that runs at 10:05 on a Monday is the one you have.

One of those is a document. The other one is your business.

Erik Hollnagel spent his career studying how complex systems hold up under strain. He gave this gap a name. Work as imagined, and work as done.

The first lives in the procedure. The second lives in the hands of the people doing it. The two drift apart in every operation, and the drift widens when the day gets loud.

Hollnagel's finding was not that people cut corners. It was that people adapt. They keep the work moving with whatever the day actually handed them.

Your team does that every Monday. They are not ignoring your process. They are absorbing the distance between your process and the day.

That absorption has a price. You just will not find it on a report.

THREE THINGS PRESSURE SHOWS YOU

The real standard. Not the one on the wall. The one people fall back to when they run out of time. Watch what gets dropped first. That drop is your actual standard, and your team already knows it.

The real owner. Watch where the work goes when it falls. If the same manager catches it every Monday, you do not have a process. You have a person. People take vacations. Processes do not.

The genuine cost. A thinned inspection is a missed recommendation. A late estimate is a compressed explanation. A compressed explanation is a declined repair that was never truly declined. The number lands in next month's gross, and nobody traces it back to 10:05 on a Monday.

The real standard. The real owner. The genuine cost. All three sit in plain view during the busiest hour of your week.

Most leaders audit the process when the store is calm. Effective leaders test it when the store is loud.

Most leaders ask, “Why didn't they follow the process?” Effective leaders ask, “What did the process ask of them at 10:05?” The first question hunts for a person to correct. The second finds a system to fix.

PEOPLE DO NOT RISE. THEY FALL BACK.

Under pressure, nobody rises to your standard. People fall back to their habits.

That is not a character flaw. That is how human beings work when the day outruns them.

So, the question is not whether your people are committed. Most of them are. The real question is what they have rehearsed, and whether your process gives them a decision they can make in four seconds.

A process that requires calm is not a process. It is a preference.

Build for the loud hour, and the quiet hour takes care of itself. Build for the quiet hour, and you will pay for it every Monday.

THE GAP BELONGS TO YOU

This is the part nobody wants.

The gap between the written process and the practiced one is not a people problem. It is a design decision, and you made it.

You staffed the drive for an average day. Mondays are not average. You built the workflow around the advisor who has been there eleven years. Half your drive has been there eleven months. You approved a tool that added a screen without removing a step.

Each call made sense on its own. Together they handed your team a process that only clears in good weather.

Three bays or thirty, the error looks the same. Leaders design for the capacity they wish they had, then grade their people against it.

Own that, and the fix gets simple. Your team is not failing your process. Your process is failing your Monday.

THE MONDAY TEST

Pick next Monday. Do not announce it.

Pick the hour. Choose the loudest sixty minutes of your week. Stand inside it. Not at your desk. On the drive.

Watch the handoff. Follow one repair order from arrival to estimate. Note every place it waits and every person who touches it.

Name the default. Write down the first thing your team dropped when the pressure hit. That is your real standard.

Then change one thing before the next Monday. One.

Do not ask whether your people followed the process. Ask whether your process survived the hour.

Pick. Watch. Name.

YOUR MOVE

This week, stop auditing the calm.

Choose your busiest hour and put yourself inside it. No clipboard. No announcement. No dashboard open on your phone.

Watch one repair order move. Count the waits. Mark the moment the process bends.

Then ask one question out loud, to the person standing in it:

What does this process ask of you when the drive is full?

Listen without defending the design. You wrote it in a quiet room. They run it in a loud one.

Then fix one thing. Remove a step, move a decision, or let someone make the call without finding you first.

Do not add a meeting. Do not add a report. Do not add a reminder to a person who is already full.

Your process is not what you wrote. It is what survives your busiest hour.
