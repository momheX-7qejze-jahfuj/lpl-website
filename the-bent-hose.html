The Bent Hose

Your team doesn’t have a communication problem. It has a pressure problem.

Written by Coach Joe|5 minute read

Picture Saturday morning. You turn on the spigot, pick up the hose, and aim it at a thirsty garden. A trickle comes out. So you do what almost everyone does. You walk back and crank the spigot harder.

Still a trickle.

You crank again. The hose swells. The fitting hisses. And out in the middle of the yard, hidden in the grass, one bend in the line is choking everything you push through it.

Now look at Monday morning. Same story. Different hose.

Your team is not starving for information. You send the emails. You run the meetings. You post the updates. The spigot is wide open. Yet the message that reaches the front line arrives thin. Distorted. Delayed. Half heard. And the old instinct kicks in. Crank it up. More meetings. More messages. More volume.

Sit with this for a second. Most communication problems are not effort problems. They are pressure problems. Somewhere in your operation there is a bend in the hose. And you cannot out-crank a kink.

I want to make three points. Only three. That is all any of us can carry, me included.

Point One: Stop cranking. Find the bend.

When communication stalls, leaders split into two schools.

The first belongs to a leader I call the Grinder. You know this leader. You have worked for this leader. On your worst weeks, you have probably been this leader. I have. The Grinder believes every flow problem is a force problem. Trickle in the field? Turn up the pressure. Add a meeting. Resend it in bold. Copy three more people. Culture trained the Grinder well: work harder, push harder, force the flow.

Here is what the Grinder never sees. Every crank raises tension in the system. The hose strains. The fittings leak. Your people feel the pressure long before they hear the message. Forcing communication through a bent channel works exactly like forcing water through a bent hose. You can do it. It just costs enormous effort, and the tension shows up everywhere else in the operation.

The second school belongs to the Pragmatist. The Pragmatist does something almost embarrassing in its simplicity. They set down the spigot handle. They walk the length of the hose. They find the bend. They straighten it. Same water. Same hose. Triple the flow.

Let me give you the same idea a second way, in language every operator understands. Think about your reporting stack. When a dashboard goes dark, you do not blame the database for lacking data. You trace the pipeline, because somewhere between the source and the screen a connection broke. The data exists. The delivery failed. Your team works the same way. The message exists. The channel is kinked.

Third way. Communication research keeps finding that in hard conversations, tone, timing, and presence carry far more meaning than the words themselves. Flatten a hard message into a text and you just bent the hose.

Stop cranking. Find the bend. Say it twice, because repetition is how ideas take root. Stop cranking. Find the bend.

Here is your moment of commitment. This week, pick the one conversation that keeps stalling on your team. Do not add volume to it. Walk the line instead. Ask one question: where exactly does this message lose pressure? That spot is your bend. Name it out loud, in front of your team.

Point Two: Standardize before you optimize.

Write this down. You have to standardize before you can optimize.

Most leaders run it backward. They chase brilliance before they build rhythm. They want the inspiring speech, the perfect offsite, the town hall that changes everything. Those are pressure spikes. A team does not run on spikes. It runs on flow. Flow comes from rhythm. Rhythm comes from standards.

Once you remove the bend, five simple rhythms keep the line open. None of them cost money. All of them cost consistency:

  1. The daily huddle. Alignment and assignments. Ten minutes. Same time. Every day. This is your pressure check at the start of the line.
  2. Review the game plan. Can you name where the gaps exist? Gaps are bends waiting to happen. Find them before they kink.
  3. Review today’s expired capacity. In my world we call them unsold hours. In yours it might be empty appointment slots, idle talent, or leads that went cold. Capacity expires at midnight. Count what you lost, honestly.
  4. Celebrate the small things. Did you notice the meaningful moments? A team that only hears from you when the flow stops learns to dread your voice.
  5. Review performance with an eye for detail. Not to punish. To learn where the line held and where it strained.

Second angle. Your huddles are your standardized inputs: boring by design, reliable by intention, powerful over time.

Third angle, and this one stings. Warren Buffett warned us: “Chains of habit are too light to be felt until they are too heavy to be broken.” He meant money. I mean your huddle. Skip it once, you feel nothing. Skip it for a quarter, your team wears a chain of silence no speech can break. But chains forge both ways. Run the huddle daily and those same links build a chain of flow. You are forging a chain right now. Choose the metal.

This is the climb. There is no adrenaline in a daily huddle. Nobody applauds a gap review on a Tuesday. This is exactly where leaders quit, right when endurance matters most. Heroic bursts will not save you, and neither will good intentions. Rhythm will. Keep climbing. The flow you want in month six gets built in the boring weeks of month one.

Standardize before you optimize. There it is again. Let it stick.

Point Three: What is rewarded is repeated.

Here is our reality. What is rewarded is repeated. What is punished is avoided.

Now read that through the lens of the hose. If your team earns praise for heroic cranking, for the late-night save, for the frantic escalation, they will crank forever. If someone quietly finds a bend, straightens it, and nobody notices, they will stop looking for bends. Without meaning to, you trained your people to force flow instead of freeing it.

Second angle. Behavioral science calls this reinforcement, and it may be the most reliable finding in the field. Behavior follows consequence. Not intention. Not the mission statement on the wall. Consequence. Your team is already perfectly trained by whatever you currently celebrate. Look at what gets repeated around you this week. That is your reward system talking, whether you designed it or not.

Third angle, the human one. Think about the last time a leader caught you doing something right, something small, and named it out loud. You still remember it. You almost certainly repeated it. Now be that leader. When someone removes a point of friction, shortens a loop, or says the hard thing face to face instead of hiding it in an email, call it out. In the huddle. By name.

What is rewarded is repeated. Third time. It is yours now.

One Small Sip

So where does this leave you?

You opened this article holding a spigot handle, cranking harder, wondering why the garden stayed dry. You leave it holding something better. A choice. Force the flow, or free it.

I will not pretend the walk down the hose is glamorous. It costs you something real. It costs the Grinder’s identity, the quiet pride of being the hardest worker in the room. It asks you to trade heroics for rhythm, volume for clarity, pressure for flow. For about two weeks, that trade feels like a loss. Then the water reaches the garden, and you wonder why you spent years standing at the spigot.

Do not fix every channel this week. Take one small sip.

Because here is the last thing I want you to carry out the door. It is better to do less than you had hoped than to do nothing at all.

Prepare the message. Practice the rhythm. Plan the flow.

Then set down the spigot handle. And go walk the hose.

Your Move

Pick the one message your team keeps missing. Now ask the question you have been ducking. Where does it lose pressure? Sit with it for sixty seconds before you reach for the spigot. The space between what you send and what they hear, that space is the bend. That space is the work.
