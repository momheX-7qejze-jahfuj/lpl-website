The Speed Bump Rule

Friction does not slow you down. It reads your speed and charges you for it.

Written by Coach Joe|5 minute read

The speed bump was invented by a Nobel Prize winner.

Not a traffic cop. Not a city council. A physicist.

Arthur Holly Compton won the 1927 Nobel Prize for explaining what happens when fast moving energy collides with something small. Physicists still call it the Compton effect. In 1945 he became Chancellor of Washington University in St. Louis. Near the end of his tenure, he watched drivers speed past Eads Hall, the physics building. Right where his students crossed.

There were signs posted. Drivers blew past them. Signs are easy to ignore at speed. Information does not slow people down. Consequence does.

So the man who spent his life studying collisions pulled out a notebook and designed one. He calculated the height. He worked out the angle. In the spring of 1953, his invention went into the pavement, and he gave it a name that tells you exactly what he understood: the traffic control bump.

He built a device that does something no sign, no policy, and no speech has ever done.

It reads your speed. Then it bills you for it.

The Genius in the Asphalt

Roll over Compton’s bump at ten miles an hour and you barely feel it. A ripple. A nod.

Hit it at thirty and it hits back. Hard.

Notice what the bump never does. It never stops anyone. It never argues, never lectures, never writes anyone up. It simply makes speed expensive at the exact spot where speed does damage. The driver still chooses. The bump just sets the price.

Seventy years later, that slab of asphalt is still working. And most leaders still lead like the drivers outside Eads Hall.

The Straightaway Trap

You know the feeling of the straightaway.

The month is tracking. The team is producing. The calendar is packed but moving. You are at top speed, and it feels like proof you are doing it right. Speed becomes the scoreboard.

Then the bump.

A comeback rolls back onto the drive. Your best advisor pushes back on the new process. A tech you counted on gives notice. A survey lands like a brick.

Watch what most leaders do next. They get mad at the bump. They treat friction as an insult, an obstacle, something the world put there to slow down someone important. So they accelerate. Handle it fast. Delegate it away. Blast through and get back up to speed.

Here is what Compton knew that they miss.

The bump is not an obstacle. It is an instrument. It measures exactly one thing: how fast you were going when you hit it.

The bump is not an obstacle. It is an instrument.

Friction Charges by Velocity

That is the rule, and it has no exceptions.

The same pushback from your advisor costs you ten minutes if you take it slow and curious. Take it at top speed and it costs you the advisor. The same comeback costs one honest conversation with a tech if you slow down for it. Blast through it and it costs a customer, a survey, and a story the whole shop tells for a month.

Friction has no fixed price. It charges by velocity. Slowing down costs seconds. Speed costs the front end.

The science behind this is uncomfortable. Princeton researchers once sent seminary students across campus to give a talk. Some had time to spare. Others were told they were already late. On the path, every one of them passed a man slumped in a doorway, clearly in distress. Most of the unhurried students stopped. Almost none of the hurried ones did. Some stepped right over him, and some were on their way to speak about the Good Samaritan.

Hurry did not change what they valued. It changed what they could see. That is what top speed does to a leader. Your vision narrows to the destination, and the periphery goes dark. The periphery is where your people live.

Where Compton Put the Bump

One more detail matters, and it might be the most important one.

Compton did not put his bump on the open road. Nobody installs speed bumps on the highway. He put it in front of his own physics building, where his students crossed. Bumps go where the people are, because that is where speed does the most damage to the things that matter most.

Your friction follows the same law. That comeback sits on top of a standard that slipped. That pushback sits on top of trust you have not yet earned for this change. That resignation sits on top of a conversation you kept postponing.

A bump is not a judgment of your driving. It is information about your location. It says: something valuable is close. Slow down here, specifically.

Bumps go where the people are.

The Speed Bump Rule

So here is the discipline I coach, and the one I still practice.

When friction hits this week, run three moves before you respond. Slow. Scan. Steer.

  1. Slow. Take ten seconds before you react. Not ten minutes. Ten seconds. Long enough to break the reflex to blast through. Speed is a choice. So is this.
  2. Scan. Ask one question: what is this bump protecting? A person. A standard. A relationship. Bumps do not appear on empty road. Find what is close.
  3. Steer. Address the thing at risk, not just the interruption. Fix the comeback, then go find the standard underneath it. Answer the pushback, then go rebuild the trust underneath it.

Most leaders resolve friction. Effective leaders read it.

And if your week has no bumps at all, do not celebrate yet. Highways are smooth because there is nothing on them to protect. A team with zero friction is often a team that stopped telling you the truth. Smooth is not always healthy. Sometimes smooth is just quiet.

Most leaders resolve friction. Effective leaders read it.

• • •

Compton is gone. His bumps are still reading drivers every day, and they never miss.

Neither does yours.

Your Move

Here is your drill for the week. Take the very next speed bump that hits your day. Before you react, finish this sentence on paper: “This bump is protecting ______.” Then act on what you wrote, not just on what interrupted you.
