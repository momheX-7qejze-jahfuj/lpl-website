You’re Stuck on a Rock, Not a Fish

What a professional bass fisherman taught me about leadership under pressure.

Written by Coach Joe|5 minute read

“You’re stuck on a rock, not a fish.”

The professional barely turned his head to say it.

I was the furthest thing from a fisherman. Truth be told, I don’t even like to touch them. But there I was, both hands on the rod, red in the face, certain I had just landed the biggest fish of the day.

I was wrong. And how I was wrong is the whole point.

The Wrong Man on the Boat

I went out with my friend Larry and his friend, a professional bass fisherman. I did not go to fish. I went to watch. A real pro, a real boat, a good story to tell. I figured I would be an observer, not a participant.

Let me be clear about my qualifications. I do not like to touch fish. I do not like to hold them, hook them, or look at them longer than good manners require. My plan for the day was simple. Stand in one spot. Nod at the right moments. Take a photo I could pretend to be proud of.

The pro had other plans. He taught me the fundamentals. He baited my first hook himself, the way you would for a child, then informed me the next one was mine.

It took me five tries to land the line where he wanted it.

Thirty seconds later, he was reeling one in. Bigger than I thought that lake could hold. A minute after that, Larry had one too. So I started reeling, grinning, sure I had mine.

I had nothing. No fish. No bait. The lake had taken both, and I never felt it happen.

For the next forty five minutes, I cast and reeled and cast and reeled. The pro kept pulling up fish and tossing them back like he was skipping stones. Casual. Easy. Almost bored.

Then I felt it. Heavy. Solid. Real.

“I got one,” I yelled.

He glanced over, watched me strain against the line, and said it flat. “You’re stuck on a rock, not a fish.”

No, I told him. This one is heavy. So I pulled harder. I leaned back. I put my shoulders into it. The line did not move an inch.

He finished tossing back what felt like his fiftieth fish of the morning. Then he strolled over. One small twist of the wrist. One easy tug. The line came free, and up came my hook. No rock. No fish. Just the thing I had been fighting with everything I had.

What I Actually Caught

Here is what I have not been able to shake since.

I caught nothing that day because I did everything harder instead of better.

The pro caught fish in thirty seconds. I fought a rock for a full minute. Same lake. Same water. Same morning. The difference was not effort. I had effort to spare. The difference was that he read the water, and I just pulled.

That is the trap of pressure. It whispers that the answer is more. More force. More grip. More strain. And more is exactly what gets you stuck.

I see it in the service drive all the time. The advisor who talks louder when the customer stops listening. The manager who bolts another process onto the last process that failed. The leader who keeps pulling on the same underperformer, year after year, and calls it patience.

The difference was not effort.

The Science Under the Surface

Psychologists have studied this for over a century. Back in 1908, two researchers named Yerkes and Dodson found that a little pressure sharpens performance, but past a certain point, more pressure breaks it down. There is a peak. Climb past it and you fall off the other side.

The pro lived at the top of that curve. Calm hands, clear eyes, room to think. I had rocketed past it, into the part where you stop thinking and start yanking.

There is more. Cognitive scientist Sian Beilock has spent her career studying why high achievers choke when the stakes rise. What she found is almost cruel. Pressure makes us watch our own hands. We take a move the body already owns, pull it back into conscious control, and strangle it with effort. She calls it explicit monitoring. Overthink the automatic, and you break it.

That was me and that rod. The pro’s wrist knew a move my whole body could not force.

Because that is what mastery actually is. Not more effort. The right small move, made calmly, built on a thousand casts you never see. What looked casual in his hands was ten thousand hours pretending to be easy.

The right small move, made calmly.

Five Moves for the Moment the Line Goes Heavy

So here is what the lake taught me. Five moves for the moments the pressure climbs.

  1. Read the water before you cast. Most leaders react. Effective leaders diagnose. Before you respond to the crisis, take sixty seconds to name what you are actually looking at. Clarity is not a delay. It is the whole cast. You cannot land a fish you never located.
  2. Loosen your grip when you feel resistance. Your instinct will be to pull harder. Fight it. Change the angle. Ease the tension. The rock does not answer to force. It answers to a twist. Coaching works the same way. When a conversation stiffens, stop pushing and change your approach.
  3. Learn the difference between a fish and a rock. Some things fight back because they are worth landing. Some things fight back because they will never move. Leaders lose years pulling on rocks and calling it commitment. Name one rock this week. Then cut the line.
  4. Let your calm set the temperature. The pro tossing fish back kept the whole boat steady, even while I flailed. Your people read your composure before they hear your words. Panic spreads. So does calm. The room borrows its temperature from the leader. Watch what you are handing out.
  5. Bait your own hook. He set me up once. After that, it was on me. Stop waiting for someone to hand you a ready line. And stop over baiting the hooks of the people you lead. Ownership either transfers, or it does not. Under pressure, you find out which.

Most leaders pull harder. Effective leaders read the water.

Leadership under pressure is not about pulling harder. It is about knowing when to pull, when to loosen, and when the heavy thing on your line is just a rock.

So the next time your line goes heavy, ask yourself one honest question before you lean back. Fish, or rock?

• • •

I never did catch a fish that day. I am at peace with that. What I caught was better. A picture of myself I do not want to repeat. Both hands on the rod, pouring everything I had into a thing that was never going to move, while the person who actually knew what he was doing freed it with a flick of the wrist.

The fish were never the test. The rock was.

Your Move

Here is your drill for the week. The next time your line goes heavy at work, stop before you pull. Name it out loud: “Fish, or rock?” One is worth landing. One you cut, and you move on.
